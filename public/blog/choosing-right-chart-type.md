# How to Pick the Right Chart

**By Marcus Rodriguez**  
**January 22, 2025 • 7 min read**  
**Tags:** guide, best-practices

---

Bar chart or line graph?

You stare at your data. You know you need to visualize it. But which chart type?

Pick wrong and your data confuses people. Pick right and they get it instantly.

Here's how to choose.

## The One Rule That Matters

Match your chart to your question.

What are you trying to show? Answer that first. Then pick the chart.

Sounds obvious, but most people do it backwards. They pick a chart they like and force their data into it.

## What Are You Trying to Show?

Every visualization answers one of four questions:

**Comparison** - Which thing is bigger? Which performed better?

**Trend** - How did this change over time? What's the pattern?

**Distribution** - How spread out is my data? Where do most values fall?

**Relationship** - Do these two things connect? When one goes up, what happens to the other?

Figure out your question. The right chart becomes obvious.

## Comparing Things: Bar Charts Win

You want to compare sales across regions. Or revenue by product. Or customer satisfaction scores by department.

Use a bar chart.

**Why bars work:** Your eye can compare heights easily. Tallest bar wins. You see the answer in one second.

**Horizontal or vertical?** Vertical works for most things. Use horizontal when you have long category names or many categories.

**When to avoid bars:** Don't use them for showing change over time. That's what line graphs do better.

## Showing Change Over Time: Use Lines

Your data has dates, months, or years. You want to show trends. Did sales go up? When did traffic spike?

Use a line graph.

**Why lines work:** Your eye follows the line and sees the direction. Up means growth. Down means decline. Flat means stable.

**Single or multiple lines?** One line is clearest. Add a second line if you need comparison. Avoid three or more lines unless absolutely necessary.

**When to avoid lines:** Don't connect points that aren't continuous. If you're comparing separate categories, use bars instead.

## Showing Parts of a Whole: Pie Charts (Maybe)

You want to show percentages that add up to 100%. Budget breakdown. Market share. Traffic sources.

Pie charts work, but people hate on them.

**Why pies get criticized:** Humans are bad at comparing angles and areas. We're better at comparing lengths.

**When pies actually work:** Use them when you have 2-4 categories and the differences are obvious. "70% of our revenue comes from product A" works great as a pie.

**Better alternative:** Try a simple bar chart instead. Seriously. Compare the bars and see which is clearer.

## Finding Relationships: Scatter Plots

You have two variables. You think they might be connected. Does advertising spending affect sales? Do older customers spend more?

Use a scatter plot.

**Why scatter works:** Each point represents one data point with two values. If they form a pattern, you've found a relationship. If they're random, there's no connection.

**Reading scatter plots:** Points trending up and to the right show positive correlation. One goes up, the other goes up. Down and right shows negative correlation.

**When to avoid scatter:** You need a decent amount of data points. Ten points won't show much. A hundred points will.

## Other Charts You'll Need Eventually

**Area charts** are filled-in line graphs. Use them to show volume over time. They emphasize the magnitude of change.

**Stacked bars** show parts of a whole across categories. Revenue by product and region combined. They get messy fast.

**Heatmaps** show patterns in large datasets using color. Website clickmaps. Sales by day and hour. Correlation matrices.

**Histograms** show distribution. How many customers fall in each age range? How are test scores distributed?

**Box plots** show statistical distribution. Median, quartiles, outliers. Great for technical audiences. Confusing for everyone else.

Start with the basics. Add these when you need them.

## How to Decide in 10 Seconds

Ask yourself:

"Am I comparing categories?" → Bar chart

"Am I showing change over time?" → Line graph

"Am I showing parts of a total?" → Pie chart or bar chart

"Am I looking for a relationship between two variables?" → Scatter plot

That covers 95% of your needs.

## Testing Your Choice

You've picked a chart type. Make it. Then ask:

**Can someone understand this in five seconds?** If they need you to explain it, try a different chart.

**Does it answer my original question?** If not, the chart type is wrong.

**Is anything confusing or misleading?** Fix it or start over.

**Would I understand this if someone showed it to me?** Be honest.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Using fancy charts to look smart.** Radar charts and bubble charts impress no one. Use them when they're truly the best choice, not because they look cool.

**Switching chart types mid-presentation.** Pick one type and stick with it when comparing similar data. Consistency helps people follow along.

**Ignoring your audience.** Technical folks can handle complex charts. Executives want simple ones. Know who you're presenting to.

**Forgetting the default option.** When in doubt, use a bar chart. They work for almost everything and everyone understands them.

## Chart Type Cheat Sheet

Keep this handy:

- **Comparing amounts:** Bar chart
- **Showing trends:** Line graph  
- **Parts of whole:** Pie chart or bar chart
- **Distribution:** Histogram
- **Relationship:** Scatter plot
- **Change in composition:** Stacked bar or area
- **Geographic data:** Map
- **Many categories over time:** Heatmap

## Practice Makes This Automatic

The more charts you make, the faster you'll know which type to use.

Try this: Take one dataset and visualize it five different ways. Which one tells the story best? That's your answer.

You'll develop intuition. Eventually you'll see data and know immediately which chart to make.

## The Real Secret

The best chart type is the one that makes your point clear.

If your boss or client has to ask "What does this show?" you picked wrong.

If they say "Oh, I see" within three seconds, you nailed it.

Start with simple charts. Master those first. Add complexity only when you need it.

Your data deserves better than being crammed into whatever chart looks prettiest. Give it the chart type that actually works.
